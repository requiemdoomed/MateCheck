MateCheck

Chess, played backwards. You begin with a king and a queen. You win when your whole army stands home again.

Play against the computer at two levels with a real chess clock, learn in a twelve-lesson tutorial, and watch the story of the king who never won.

How the realm runs backwards

Real chess starts with full armies and ends with a lonely king. MateCheck turns that around. Every game starts where a lost game would end, and you play it back to the opening position.

  1. Begin with a crown

    Each side starts with only its king and queen. The other fourteen pieces wait in the reserve.

  2. Rally the fallen

    When one of your pieces leaves a square on your muster ground, a fallen comrade can rise on the square it left.

  3. Walk them home

    Comrades move like chess pieces, and pawns walk backwards. A piece that reaches its own starting square is sealed there for good.

  4. Call MateCheck

    The first army to stand complete in the classic starting position wins the game.

The laws of the reversed realm

What stays from chess

  • Knights, bishops, rooks, queens and kings move exactly as you know them.
  • Check is still check. You may never leave your own king attacked.
  • A bishop never changes colour, so the reserve keeps a light one and a dark one.
  • Pieces block each other as usual. Only knights jump.

What flips

  • Pawns walk backwards, one step straight or diagonal toward their own second rank. From the fourth rank a pawn may step two at once.
  • Only royals strike. A king or queen can banish an enemy comrade back to its reserve. Nobody dies, and comrades never strike or give check.
  • Comrades shield each other. In its own half, a comrade with a friend next to it can't be banished.
  • Checkmate is outlawed. A move that would leave a king with no escape is simply not allowed.
  • Courts are closed. No piece may enter the enemy's two home ranks.
  • Recall sends one of your own unsealed comrades back to the reserve, for when a piece gets stuck.
  • Dawn breaks after 100 moves each. Whoever has more pieces home wins.
  • Or play the Grand Revival. Raise every comrade and walk them home against the clock. When time runs out, nobody loses on time: the bigger revival wins.

Classic ending

MateCheck at Dawn

Stand all sixteen pieces on their starting squares to win on the spot. If nobody manages it, dawn breaks after 100 moves each and more pieces home wins. With a clock, running out of time loses.

The original idea

The Grand Revival

Every fallen piece must be revived and brought back to its square. MateCheck still wins at once, but when a clock runs out (or dawn breaks) the revival is counted: 1 point for every comrade risen onto the board, 1 more for every piece standing home, up to 30. The bigger revival wins.

One piece of advice before you play: fill your back rank first. Sealed pawns never move again, so a pawn wall closed too early locks your rooks, bishops and royals out for good.

The cast

The stage door. Open it and the show begins.

The script. A Jupyter notebook that holds every line of game code, tunes the computer opponent by self-play, composes the soundtrack and writes all of it into assets/.

sound.js
Gapless music loops, the travelling gameplay soundtrack, crossfades and live synthesized sound effects.

Behind the curtain

  • Search Negamax with alpha-beta and principal variation search, iterative deepening, a 262,144-entry transposition table keyed by 64-bit Zobrist hashes, killer and history move ordering, late move reductions, check extensions and a quiescence search over banishing moves.
  • Evaluation "Work remaining": for every piece, the true number of moves it still needs to reach home, found by breadth-first search around its own sealed pieces. Path maps are cached per wall pattern, so a locked-out rook costs a fortune and the computer avoids sealing itself in.
  • Two endings MateCheck at Dawn, or the Grand Revival, where a falling flag ends the match and the army with the higher revival score (comrades risen plus pieces home) wins. The computer weighs that score more heavily as its clock runs down.
  • Two opponents Beginner-friendly looks one move ahead for each side and sometimes picks a weaker idea on purpose. Expert Courtroom searches up to eight half-moves deep within about a second and plays to win.
  • Rules safety net 42 automated rule checks, 400 random games per test run and dozens of computer-against-computer matches, with a Python twin of the engine that agrees with it move for move on every test position.

The orchestra

  • Nineteen cues composed in code: a royal synthwave hall theme, a full-band adventure overture for the prologue, a taiko-and-flute theme for the old realm, a rain-soaked lament, a heavenly choir, a cliffhanger, a tutorial theme, six exploration themes for matches, a battle cue and four stingers.
  • The journey During a match the music keeps travelling: each exploration theme (sea, desert, forest, sky, hidden village, mountains) plays for about a minute before the next crossfades in, and checks, a close finish or a low clock cut to the battle cue.
  • Instruments Built from scratch with NumPy and SciPy: additive harps, harpsichords and pianos, FM bells and electric piano, formant-filtered choirs, band-limited brass and horn, taiko, koto, bamboo flute, accordion, overdriven guitar, synthesized drums and convolution reverb.
  • Playback 128 kbps MP3, looped sample-accurately through the Web Audio API with crossfades between scenes. Move sounds are synthesized live in the browser.

Running notes

  • Stack HTML5, CSS3 and plain JavaScript with no frameworks and no build step. Web Audio, Web Workers, and Speech Synthesis for the optional narrator.
  • Runs anywhere Recent Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari (15.4 or newer), on desktop and phone. Works straight from your disk, offline, or from GitHub Pages.
  • Rebuild it Python 3.10+, NumPy, SciPy and lameenc, then run all cells in MateCheck.ipynb.
  • Saves Your settings and an unfinished game are kept in your browser's local storage. Nothing leaves your device.
